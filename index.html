Abstract

Reliable long-horizon prediction remains a central challenge for embodied world models. Although recent generative models can synthesize visually plausible action-conditioned futures, their latent dynamics are typically learned through unconstrained transition modules, which may gradually deviate from coherent physical evolution as predictions are recursively rolled forward. We investigate whether explicitly structuring latent state evolution can improve the stability and utility of such imagined trajectories.

To this end, we introduce HWM, a structured dynamics framework that represents backbone latents in a learned phase space consisting of generalized coordinates and momenta. Future states are propagated through dissipative Hamiltonian dynamics and mapped back to the original latent representation through a learnable inverse interface. The resulting formulation can be integrated into existing world-model architectures without redesigning their perception or decoding components, while multi-step transition supervision encourages the structured rollout to remain aligned with the latent trajectories induced by real observations.

Controlled simulation experiments show that HWM produces more accurate estimates of velocity, acceleration, and jerk and substantially reduces long-horizon energy drift relative to unstructured latent dynamics. We further evaluate the approach across three distinct embodied world-model settings, covering single-arm multi-view prediction with Ctrl-World, dual-arm single-view prediction with EVAC, and task-oriented single-arm prediction with Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B. On RoboCasa, incorporating HWM into Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B yields a 2.7% relative improvement in task success. On real-world AgiBot World trajectories, it improves nDTW by 9.7% with Ctrl-World and Logic by 8.4% with EVAC. Across these settings, the results indicate that introducing explicit dynamical structure into latent evolution can improve both long-horizon predictive stability and the downstream usefulness of embodied world-model rollouts.

Model Architecture

Given an observation \(o_t\), the encoder \(E_\phi\) maps it to a latent \(z_t\). HWM lifts \(z_t\) into generalized coordinates and momenta \((q_t, p_t)\) and rolls them forward with a learned Hamiltonian \(H_\theta(q,p)=\tfrac{1}{2}p^\top M_\theta^{-1}(q)\,p+V_\theta(q)\) and a semi-implicit symplectic Euler step. Predicted phase states are mapped back to backbone latents and decoded to future observations. Training combines world-model reconstruction, phase-space reconstruction, and multi-step transition consistency.

Conclusion

HWM places physical structure in the latent transition itself. Phase variables evolve under controlled dissipative Hamiltonian dynamics, with a symplectic rollout and a residual path back to the backbone latent space. Across simulation, Ctrl-World, EVAC, and Cosmos, this changes predicted motion, energy drift, visual-temporal metrics, and downstream task success without replacing the renderer or the tokenizer.

The experiments do not involve human subjects or deployment on physical robots. A more physically consistent world model can make model-based evaluation more faithful, and it still needs the same care as any component used for autonomous control.
